Solo female travel in India: where to go and what the safety advice says

The UK government's travel advice for India warns that women travelers often face harassment, and that sexual assaults have been reported, including attacks on foreign women in tourist areas and cities. This page sets out that advice and what we found for women traveling alone: hostels with female-only dorms in the backpacker towns, women's coaches on the Delhi Metro and Indian Railways, and the national helplines, each with its source. In an emergency anywhere in India, call 112.

We make Tablamigo, which has women-only plans, so weigh that. Facts come from the sources linked on this page, checked on 11 October 2026 unless a different date is given. Where we give our own opinion, we say so. If you've traveled these routes and something here is wrong or missing, write to support@hookstep.in.

The short answer

The UK government's travel advice for India, updated on 7 October 2026, is the most detailed official advice we found. It says "Female travellers often experience verbal and physical harassment by individuals or groups of men," and warns that serious sexual attacks have been reported, including against foreign women in tourist areas and cities. Its practical advice:

  • Avoid isolated areas, including beaches, when you're alone, at any time of day.
  • Don't accept food or drinks from strangers on trains, and be wary of accepting them anywhere else, or of leaving a drink unattended.
  • "Avoid travelling alone and hailing taxis on the street, especially at night." App taxis such as Uber and Ola are widely available, including at every airport terminal, and so are prepaid taxis. If you book online, there's usually a way to share the journey with your contacts.

Source: UK Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office, India: safety and security, updated 7 October 2026 and shown as still current on 11 October 2026. The US State Department's India advisory returned an error when we checked, so it isn't quoted here.

Our view: start where there are lots of other travelers and a hostel with a female-only dorm, arrive in daylight, and meet new people somewhere public before you go anywhere with them. That's our judgement, not an official rating.

Where to go

These are the places in our guides where we found at least one hostel with a female-only dorm on the hostel's own website. Each guide has the hostels, meeting spots and local safety tips.

  • Rishikesh: yoga, cafés and the Ganges. Two hostels with female-only dorms.
  • McLeod Ganj: Tibetan culture, drop-in meditation and the Triund trek. Two hostels with female-only dorms.
  • North Goa: the beaches at Anjuna, Vagator and Arambol. Two hostels with female-only dorms. The UK advice says to avoid isolated areas, including beaches, when you're alone, at any time of day.
  • Udaipur and Jaipur: lakes, palaces and forts. One hostel with a female-only dorm in each.
  • Varanasi: the ghats, and the Ganga aarti at Dashashwamedh Ghat, held every evening at dusk (Wikipedia). One hostel with female-only dorms. Our view: the aarti is easier with company.
  • Hampi and Gokarna: ruins and beaches in Karnataka. Gokarna has two hostels with female-only dorms. Hampi has one, about 23 km away in Gangavathi.
  • Manali, Kasol, Leh-Ladakh and Bir Billing: the mountains, with one hostel with a female-only dorm in each. Foreign tourists have gone missing in and around the Parvati Valley, where Kasol is, since the early 1990s (Wikipedia). The UK advice says trekkers should travel in groups, use reputable local guides, stay on established routes and leave their plans with their accommodation.
  • Delhi: two hostels with female-only dorms, and women-only coaches on the metro.

Getting around

  • Delhi Metro. Trains have coaches reserved for women. The Delhi Metro fined 2,320 men for entering them in 2024–25, at ₹250 each, according to The Tribune (20 July 2025). Metro penalties changed on 19 June 2026 under the Jan Vishwas Act (Gazette of India), and we couldn't confirm the new amount.
  • Trains. Indian Railways has women-only coaches and berths reserved for women, and the spot fine for men who enter them has gone up from ₹500 to ₹2,500, Madhyamam reported on 3 October 2026. It says the increase comes from the Jan Vishwas Act, whose changes to the Railways Act came into force on 19 June 2026 (Gazette of India). The UK advice adds that there are police or guards on trains and at stations if you need help, and the railway's RailMadad service lists 139 for security or medical help.
  • Taxis. The UK advice (above) says to avoid hailing taxis on the street, that app and prepaid taxis are widely available, and that an app booking usually lets you share the journey.
  • Overnight buses. Buses from Delhi to the hills often run overnight. An evening bus to Bhuntar, the stop for Kasol, gets in around 7 to 9 am, according to this Kasol travel guide (updated 17 August 2026), and the same writer's McLeod Ganj guide says most operators to Dharamshala run overnight Volvo and semi-sleeper buses. That's road travel at night, which the UK advice says to avoid. Our view: if you take the overnight bus anyway, book a known operator and have a bed booked for when you arrive.

Helplines

  • 112 is India's national emergency number for police, fire and ambulance, with help in English and other languages (UK FCDO, getting help). The Ministry of Women and Child Development also lists 112 for emergencies.
  • 181 is the Women Helpline, run under the government's Mission Shakti scheme. It answers around the clock and puts callers in touch with the police, One Stop Centres, hospitals and legal services (Ministry of Women and Child Development). It isn't run in West Bengal, according to The Reporters' Collective (3 October 2024), so use 112 there.
  • 139 is the railway number for security or medical help on a train (RailMadad).
  • 1091 is listed as a women helpline in the UK government's advice. We couldn't confirm that it answers in every state, so save 112 first.

Save them in your phone before you travel. Tablamigo can't call emergency services or contact anyone for you.

Common situations

  • Comments, groping or someone pressing close in a crowd. The UK advice says women travelers often face verbal and physical harassment. If you feel unsafe, call 112, or 139 on a train. Our view: move towards other women or families, or into the women's coach on the Delhi Metro or a train.
  • A stranger offers food or a drink. The UK advice says not to accept food or drinks from strangers on trains, and to be wary of accepting them anywhere else or leaving a drink unattended. It reports spiked drinks and food, with British travelers robbed or assaulted.
  • Visiting a temple, ashram or ghat. The UK advice asks travelers to respect the local dress code at religious sites and in rural areas.
  • Arriving late. The UK advice says to avoid road travel at night, when accidents are common and the risk of crime is higher. It also says app and prepaid taxis are available at airports, and many hotels offer transfers. Our view: if you can't avoid a late arrival, book the bed before you land and take one of those.
  • Drugs. Charas and ganja (cannabis resin and flowers) are illegal under India's Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances Act (Wikipedia), including in the Parvati Valley, which is known for locally grown cannabis. The UK advice says not to get involved with any illegal drugs: possession of a small amount for personal use could mean at least 6 months in prison, and you could be held for years while the case is processed.

Questions

Is India safe for solo female travelers?

The UK government's advice says women travelers often face verbal and physical harassment, and it warns of serious sexual attacks. Its advice is to avoid isolated places when alone, not to accept food or drinks from strangers on trains, and to avoid hailing taxis on the street, especially at night. In an emergency, call 112. Our view: hostels with female-only dorms on the main routes, and meeting new people in public first, make a first trip easier.

Which city is safest for a first solo trip as a woman?

No official source ranks Indian cities for this, so we won't. Our own judgement: a first trip is easier in a town full of other travelers, with short distances and a hostel with a female-only dorm, such as Rishikesh, McLeod Ganj or North Goa. Our guides list the hostels we checked in each.

Are hostels in India safe for women?

No official source rates hostels for safety, so we can't say. What we checked: this page lists the hostels in our India guides whose own websites list a female-only dorm. Our view: pick one with recent reviews from women, use the locker, and meet new people in the common areas first.

Is South India safer for women travelers?

We haven't found an official source that compares regions, so we can't say. The UK advice applies to the whole country. Our guides cover Gokarna and Hampi in Karnataka and Goa on the west coast, each with its own safety tips.

What is the women's helpline number in India?

In an emergency, call 112, India's national emergency number. 181 is the government's Women Helpline and answers around the clock everywhere except West Bengal, which doesn't run it. On a train, 139 is the railway number for security or medical help. The UK government's advice also lists 1091 as a women helpline, but we couldn't confirm that it answers in every state.
